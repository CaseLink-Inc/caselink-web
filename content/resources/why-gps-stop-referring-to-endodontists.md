## How much of an endo practice rests on a handful of GPs?

Nearly all of it. The American Association of Endodontists' 2026 referral patterns survey found that about 90 percent of the root canals endodontists perform start as a referral from another dentist ([AAE, 2026](https://newsroom.aae.org/featured-stories/more-dentists-are-referring-to-endodontists-new-aae-survey-finds/)). The average endodontist reported 1,930 referred patients a year.

The trend has been in the specialty's favor. In 2012, 43 percent of root canal cases were referred to an endodontist. In the 2026 survey the figure was 53 percent. Ninety-two percent of general dentists said they have a positive view of endodontists.

The number that matters for this piece sits further down. Eighty percent of endodontists said they had gained new referring dentists in recent years. Sixty-five percent said they had lost at least one.

{{aae}}

Put those two together and you get a practice that can be growing and leaking at once. New GPs arrive, the monthly referral count holds or rises, and nobody notices that a dentist who used to send six cases a quarter has sent none since spring. (The AAE survey was supported by Sonendo, and the release doesn't give a sample size. We'd treat the percentages as directional.)

## Why do GPs say they stop referring?

This is where the 2026 study in the *Journal of Endodontics* gets useful. Researchers at Temple University surveyed 793 endodontists and 559 referring dentists and asked both groups the same questions ([Karia, Hong and Kim, *J Endod*, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joen.2026.04.013)).

On why they refer in the first place, the two groups agreed. A clinical procedural problem was the main driver for 47.2 percent of GPs and 50.7 percent of endodontists. Calcified canals, retreatment, a case that went sideways. Nothing surprising there.

On why a referral relationship ends, they didn't agree at all. Endodontists most often named divergent treatment philosophy, at 40.0 percent. Referring dentists most often named patient dissatisfaction, at 30.7 percent.

{{reasons}}

That gap matters. An endodontist who thinks GPs leave over clinical disagreement will respond with clinical arguments. Case presentations, CE evenings, a lunch to talk through retreatment criteria. All reasonable things. None of them reach the problem the GP actually named.

One caveat. The survey went out through professional networks and social media, so respondents chose to take part. It's the best recent dual-perspective data on this question, and its sample is large, but it isn't a random draw of every US dentist.

## What does the patient tell the GP afterwards?

Here's a version of the conversation, as an illustration. It isn't a case from the study.

A patient comes back to her GP for a crown on tooth 19, six weeks after the root canal. The hygienist asks how it went. She says the treatment was fine, but she waited nine days to get in, sat for 40 minutes past her appointment time, and left without a clear idea of what came next. She assumed the endodontist would "send something." She isn't sure anything was sent.

Her root canal may well have been excellent. The GP will still hear about the nine days, the 40 minutes and the note that never arrived.

The study points at one specific place where this goes wrong. Thirty percent of referring dentists said restorability assessment is mainly the endodontist's responsibility. Only 20.6 percent of endodontists agreed. When each office believes the other owns the decision, the patient can leave the endo chair without anyone having said plainly what happens to the tooth next. The GP then has to reconstruct it from a report and a patient who half remembers.

The authors' own conclusion names "bidirectional communication of restorative intent" as one of two fixes. That phrase is worth taking literally. Write the restorative next step down, give it to the patient, and send the same line to the GP.

## Is the referral letter only the GP's problem?

The study found the paperwork problem runs in one direction. Referring dentists reported high satisfaction with the reports endodontists send back. Endodontists didn't return the favor. In the same survey, 44.2 percent of them said the referral letters they receive are inadequate more than 30 percent of the time.

{{letters}}

So information flows well from the specialist to the GP, and poorly from the GP to the specialist. That shapes the patient's first visit. If the letter is missing the reason for referral, recent radiographs or the GP's restorative plan, the endodontist's team spends the appointment rebuilding the case. The patient feels the delay, and that delay is part of the story they'll tell later.

## How do GPs want to hear from you?

By phone, mostly. Asked how they prefer to maintain the referral relationship, 51.4 percent of referring dentists chose phone calls. Only 25.7 percent of endodontists picked the phone as their top channel.

Office visits flip the pattern. They were valued by 18.2 percent of endodontists and only 7.4 percent of GPs.

{{channels}}

Many endo practices spend real money and doctor time on the lunch-and-learn circuit. The data suggests GPs would trade some of that for a three-minute call when something about their patient needs a decision. A call about a live case also gives the GP something they can't get from a catered lunch: news about a specific person in their care.

## What changes when the GP's group hires its own endodontist?

For an independent endodontist, the competition for a GP's referrals increasingly comes from inside the GP's own organization.

At the Yankee Multi-Site Summit in January 2026, Simply Dental Management described rebuilding its internal referral pathways as outside lead flow softened. It added periodontists and used traveling oral surgeons, and general dentists "were held accountable for internal referrals" ([Group Dentistry Now, February 2026](https://www.groupdentistrynow.com/?p=31106)).

Endodontics is moving the same way. PDS Health has built endodontic and periodontic groups embedded inside its general practices, a model described as "eliminating the need for off-site referrals" ([Group Dentistry Now, April 2025](https://www.groupdentistrynow.com/?p=28312)).

An independent practice can't win on convenience against an endodontist down the hall. What it can control is how the patient feels about the visit and how little effort the GP has to spend finding out what happened. Those are the two things the *J Endod* data says GPs care about when they decide to stay or go.
